Autarky: The Country That Built Its Own Lifeboat

From strategic semi-autarky to a consumer economy. How an Afrikaner-led government built South Africa's industrial independence, and how much of that strength has been lost since 1994.

HISTORY & HERITAGE · INDUSTRY & ECONOMY · 27 OCTOBER 2026

On 4 November 1977, the United Nations imposed a mandatory arms embargo on South Africa. Foreign governments were prohibited from supplying it with weapons, ammunition and military equipment.

The Afrikaner-led government faced a choice. Remain dependent on countries that could cut off supplies, or develop the ability to manufacture what the country needed.

It chose to manufacture.

By 1990, South Africa's defence industry employed approximately 130,000 people across 3,000 companies. It manufactured artillery, armoured vehicles, ammunition and increasingly sophisticated military systems.

An international embargo intended to restrict military power had accelerated the development of an indigenous defence industry.

But weapons were only one part of a much bigger national undertaking.

Economists have a word for what South Africa pursued: semi-autarky.

Autarky means complete economic self-sufficiency. Semi-autarky is the more practical ambition of maintaining enough domestic production to withstand disruptions to essential imports while continuing to trade internationally.

Food. Fuel. Steel. Electricity. Defence.

The objective was national resilience. If foreign suppliers closed their doors, the country's essential industries would keep operating.

This ambition began long before international sanctions reached their height.

In 1928, Prime Minister J.B.M. Hertzog's government established Iscor, a state-owned steel producer. On 4 April 1934, the first steel was tapped at its Pretoria works.

Steel meant railway lines, bridges, factories, mining equipment, vehicles and weapons. It was the foundation upon which an industrial nation could stand.

Then came fuel.

South Africa possessed enormous coal reserves but little commercially viable crude oil. A country dependent on imported petroleum could see its transport, agriculture and military operations threatened by foreign supply restrictions.

In 1950, the government established Sasol to produce synthetic fuel from coal, using and developing technology based on the German Fischer-Tropsch process.

Following the 1973 oil crisis, the government backed a massive expansion at Secunda. Sasol Two began producing synthetic fuel in 1980, followed by Sasol Three in 1982.

Consider that achievement.

A country with almost no oil had developed industrial facilities capable of converting its own coal into petrol and diesel. By 1988, Sasol reported supplying approximately 27% of national fuel requirements.

The government also accumulated enormous strategic petroleum reserves. A 1996 Mail & Guardian investigation reported that the underground storage facilities at Ogies had once held 118 million barrels, estimated to be enough for approximately a year.

Electricity followed the same philosophy. The national grid relied overwhelmingly on domestically mined coal, while Koeberg, the French-designed nuclear power station near Cape Town, entered commercial operation in 1984 and 1985.

South Africa also developed uranium-enrichment capabilities and constructed six nuclear weapons, with a seventh incomplete.

And an extraordinary detail is frequently overlooked: President F.W. de Klerk's National Party government dismantled the entire nuclear arsenal before the ANC assumed power in 1994.

Missile technology had reached flight-testing, although South Africa's first satellite would only reach orbit in 1999 aboard an American rocket.

Agriculture provided another layer of resilience. South Africa developed into a major agricultural producer and exporter, supported by extensive farming, food-processing and distribution industries.

Local ingenuity reached ordinary households too. Bokomo launched ProNutro in 1962, an example of domestic food research becoming a recognisable South African product.

These achievements were not exclusively Afrikaner inventions. Engineers, scientists, farmers and workers from different communities contributed, while foreign expertise remained important. But the strategic industrialisation programme was substantially directed and financed by an Afrikaner-led state.

It was expensive. Some industries required heavy subsidies, international isolation restricted economic growth, and the political system denied most South Africans equal political rights.

Nevertheless, the industrial capabilities were real.

Then came 1994. And over the following three decades, the balance between producing and purchasing changed dramatically.

In July 2026, ANC Trade and Industry Minister Parks Tau acknowledged the scale of the defence industry's decline.

The approximately 130,000 jobs across 3,000 companies in 1990 had fallen to around 13,000 across 600 firms.

A 90% reduction in employment.

In November 2025, steel production at ArcelorMittal's Newcastle works ceased.

In September 2026, the South African Reserve Bank reported that domestic refining capacity had halved over the preceding decade, ending the country's near self-sufficiency in liquid fuels. Imported refined products now supplied more than half of domestic demand.

Minister Gwede Mantashe confirmed strategic reserves of approximately 6.9 million barrels, equivalent to around 30 days of crude oil requirements.

The broader manufacturing figures tell the same story.

In 1994, manufacturing contributed approximately 21% of South Africa's GDP. By 2025, according to World Bank data, the figure had declined to approximately 12.3%.

And in 2025, imported vehicles accounted for 69.1% of new light-vehicle sales.

Meanwhile, the number of large shopping centres exceeding 30,000 square metres grew from 36 in 1994 to 160 in 2014.

Those malls were largely private investments, and they created jobs. But they illustrate a profound economic distinction.

A shopping mall distributes what factories produce. It cannot replace the factories.

A country can have magnificent shopping centres, international brands and warehouses full of imported products while losing the industrial capacity required to manufacture essential goods itself.

For Americans, the principle should sound familiar. The industrial strength associated with Pittsburgh's steelworks, Detroit's automobile factories and the country's great engineering enterprises was built on productive capacity, skilled labour and technological ambition.

Americans who worry about losing domestic factories to foreign competitors understand the danger of relying on overseas suppliers for strategic necessities.

The same principle applies to Afrikaners.

There is nothing shameful about international trade. Nor should South Africa attempt complete autarky. The country still possesses substantial industrial capabilities, Sasol continues producing fuel, Koeberg generates electricity, and agricultural exports reached a record US$15.1 billion in 2025.

Some industrial decline also began before 1994, and global competition has challenged manufacturers across the world.

But those realities do not erase the responsibilities of successive ANC-led governments, or the measurable erosion of major industries during their years in office.

The distinction is between strategic semi-autarky and an increasingly consumer-oriented economy.

One invested heavily in the capacity to produce essential goods. The other has become more dependent on purchasing important goods from abroad.

A nation's prosperity cannot be judged by the number of international brands displayed in its shopping centres. True national resilience also depends on factories, power stations, refineries, farms, engineers and the skills passed from one generation to the next.

The Afrikaner-led state understood that principle and invested heavily in it.

Its successors inherited much of that industrial foundation. In several crucial sectors, they have failed to preserve its strength.

A nation that can manufacture what it needs has choices. A nation that can only buy what it needs must hope someone is willing to sell.
