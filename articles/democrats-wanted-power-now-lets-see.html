The Democrats Wanted Power. Now Let’s See What They Do With It.

It is easy to criticise from the sidelines, promise to do things better and blame the government for everything that goes wrong. Now the Democrats have more power, the responsibility is theirs.

For years, Democrats have argued that Donald Trump and the Republicans were the obstacle to better government. They promised that a change in congressional power would bring a different direction. The 2026 midterms have given them a stronger hand. The argument now moves from campaigning to accountability.

Winning seats is not the same as governing. The next two years will show whether the party can turn its promises into legislation, oversight and practical results, or whether its energy will be spent primarily on opposing the White House.

Power brings responsibility

Congress has real tools. A majority can shape committee agendas, hold hearings, investigate executive decisions, influence spending legislation and decide which bills reach the floor. Control of the House is particularly significant for investigations and appropriations. Senate control matters for confirmations and the passage of legislation.

But congressional power has limits. Donald Trump remains President. He can veto legislation passed by Congress, and overriding a veto requires a two-thirds majority in both chambers. A Democratic majority therefore cannot simply enact its entire programme over presidential opposition.

That distinction matters. The Democrats can block, scrutinise, negotiate and legislate where they can assemble the votes. They cannot govern as though they hold the presidency as well.

What should voters look for?

First, whether the party focuses on issues that materially affect Americans: the cost of living, jobs, public safety, healthcare, energy and the federal budget. Second, whether its investigations produce evidence and accountability rather than becoming permanent political theatre. Third, whether it is willing to negotiate when a workable compromise is possible.

Oversight is a legitimate function of Congress. It is not automatically obstruction. Equally, an endless sequence of hearings that produces no clear findings or policy outcomes will not answer the problems voters expect elected representatives to address.

The veto is not a footnote

Any claim that Democrats can simply reverse presidential policy by passing a bill overlooks the constitutional process. A bill must pass both chambers in identical form and be presented to the President. A veto can be overridden only with the required supermajorities. Where those votes do not exist, Democrats will need to negotiate, use oversight powers or pursue changes that fall within Congress's authority.

This is why the distinction between political messaging and governing capacity matters. A party can promise a great deal while campaigning. In office, it must explain what it can actually deliver.

The next two years will be the test

Republicans will no longer be able to carry the same responsibility for congressional outcomes if Democrats control the relevant chamber. Democrats will have to defend their own priorities, their spending decisions and the results of their investigations. They will also have to explain what they chose not to pursue.

The electorate has given them influence. It has not given them unlimited power. The President retains the veto, the Constitution still divides authority, and voters will judge both parties on what happens next.

The Democrats wanted power. Now let’s see what they do with it.

Editorial note: The balance of power and individual race calls should be checked against certified results as state canvasses conclude. The constitutional description above reflects the ordinary federal legislative and veto process.
