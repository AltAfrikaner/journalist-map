Tom Eaton Warns of a Trojan Horse. Common Sense Says It's Already Inside.

An American tech genius offers satellite internet to rural schools, and suddenly foreign influence is a national emergency. Meanwhile, Chinese state-backed technology receives official attention, international lenders negotiate reforms, and Soros-funded organisations have operated in South Africa for decades. Apparently, a Trojan horse is only dangerous when the wrong billionaire owns it.

OPINION · SOUTH AFRICA · TECHNOLOGY & POLITICS

There is something wonderfully entertaining about watching South Africa's progressive commentariat suddenly discover the dangers of foreign influence.

Not all foreign influence, naturally.

Just the kind associated with Elon Musk.

In his column, "Common Sense would tell you Starlink is a Trojan horse", Tom Eaton warns South Africans against allowing Musk's satellite network into the country.

A giant wooden horse, apparently, offering something useful while concealing something sinister.

Very dramatic.

Except somebody forgot to check whether the gates were already open.

Foreign foundations have funded South African institutions for decades. Chinese technology companies are negotiating cooperation agreements with government. International lenders finance programmes tied to negotiated policy reforms.

And George Soros's Open Society Foundation for South Africa was established in 1993.

Before the country's first democratic elections.

The horse arrived long before Starlink.

The question is not whether every foreign organisation is dangerous.

The question is why Eaton's concern about foreign influence appears to begin and end with Musk.

The Far Left's Billionaire Problem

Eaton seems particularly fascinated by Musk's wealth.

Trillionaire this. Trillionaire that.

Perhaps the irritation is not that wealthy businessmen influence society.

Perhaps it is that the billionaire whose politics he dislikes has become considerably wealthier than the billionaire associated with causes he might find more agreeable.

George Soros committed more than $32 billion to his Open Society Foundations.

Musk built businesses involving rockets, satellites, electric vehicles and telecommunications.

Soros built an international network financing research, advocacy, civil society and institutional reform.

Both have money.

Both have influence.

Both deserve scrutiny.

But the argument becomes suspiciously selective when one billionaire's spending is automatically celebrated as philanthropy while another billionaire's proposed investment is treated as evidence of a sinister agenda.

Apparently, the wealth gap has finally become intolerable.

For those of us in the political centre, the issue is rather simpler.

We do not need to worship Musk.

We do not need to defend his every decision.

And we certainly do not need to adopt his politics to recognise the possible value of affordable satellite internet.

We can demand accountability and welcome innovation simultaneously.

What a revolutionary concept.

After more than thirty years of ANC-led government, ordinary South Africans have considerably more pressing questions.

Why does infrastructure fail?

Why is public procurement so vulnerable to corruption?

Why do functioning railways, reliable electricity and basic municipal services remain such difficult achievements?

And why must every proposal for improving conditions first pass through an ideological loyalty test?

Perhaps that is what really threatens the political establishment.

Not that a different billionaire has accumulated greater wealth.

But that voters might start demanding measurable results instead of another thirty years of explanations.

A Communist Trojan Horse Gets a Welcome Meeting

In July 2025, South African Communications Minister Solly Malatsi met representatives of the China Satellite Network Company.

This is a Chinese state-owned enterprise developing satellite broadband services.

The discussions concerned internet access for rural and underserved communities.

Satellite internet.

Rural connectivity.

Closing the digital divide.

That sounds rather familiar.

It is broadly what Starlink proposes.

Except the Chinese company is owned by a state governed by the Chinese Communist Party.

Starlink is a privately owned American company.

To be fair, Malatsi is a DA minister. He has engaged with several providers, including Starlink, and no Chinese satellite operator has been shown to enjoy a special licensing exemption.

But the comparison raises an obvious question.

If foreign control of communications infrastructure is dangerous, shouldn't a satellite system owned by the Chinese state face at least equally rigorous scrutiny?

And China's relationship with South African technology policy extends further.

In 2025, ANC minister Blade Nzimande's department signed a technology cooperation agreement with Huawei.

In July 2026, Nzimande reaffirmed cooperation involving artificial intelligence, cloud computing, research and advanced communications networks.

Huawei and China Satellite Network Company are different organisations. Their commercial and ownership arrangements should not be confused.

But both relationships deserve scrutiny concerning cybersecurity, operational independence, data protection and reliance on foreign infrastructure.

Who controls the equipment?

Who controls the network?

Where is sensitive data processed?

What happens if international relations deteriorate?

And who decides whether South African users remain connected?

If Eaton is genuinely concerned about foreign influence over communications, those questions cannot apply exclusively to American companies.

A satellite does not become safer because its owner answers to Beijing rather than Texas.

Perhaps the Trojan horse simply looks less threatening when it arrives wearing the correct ideological colours.

Five Thousand Schools. One Very Convenient Distraction.

Eaton mocks Elon Musk's proposed investment in South African education.

US ambassador Brent Bozell spoke of 500 schools. Starlink's published proposal identifies 5,000.

That discrepancy deserves correcting.

But Eaton turns it into another opportunity for sarcasm while acknowledging that the available accounts give different figures.

The company's formal proposal is clear.

5,000 rural schools.

R500 million.

More than 2.4 million learners potentially benefiting annually.

Starlink proposes free connectivity together with equipment and supporting services. Its published position describes a long-term commitment.

Of course, a proposal is not a completed programme.

Government should demand enforceable terms, independent monitoring, technical safeguards and clear provisions for continuity of service.

But those are practical questions.

Eaton seems considerably more entertained by how quickly Musk can earn R500 million.

Apparently, philanthropy becomes less worthwhile as the donor becomes wealthier.

By that logic, a schoolchild should be less grateful for an internet connection because the billionaire supplying it can afford the expense.

How reassuring.

The child has no internet, but at least somebody has explained why the donor is insufficiently admirable.

And this brings us to a familiar feature of South Africa's political commentary.

Privileged progressives with reliable internet connections are remarkably comfortable deciding what people without reliable internet should receive.

They have the platforms.

They have the audiences.

They have the luxury of debating ideology online.

Meanwhile, a rural teacher may simply want to download educational resources without battling unreliable connectivity.

A pupil may want access to material readily available to children in wealthier communities.

Those voices are conspicuously absent from Eaton's argument.

The well-connected speak for the disconnected.

As they so often do.

Apparently, deciding what disadvantaged communities need is another service the progressive establishment provides without first asking them.

BEE: Who Actually Benefits?

Eaton also invokes Broad-Based Black Economic Empowerment.

This is where the debate becomes considerably more interesting than another joke about Musk's wealth.

South Africa's telecommunications licensing framework includes ownership requirements intended to benefit historically disadvantaged South Africans.

Starlink wants an alternative arrangement through an Equity Equivalent Investment Programme.

Such programmes are recognised within the broader B-BBEE framework and allow qualifying investments to fulfil empowerment objectives without necessarily transferring the same ownership stake.

The dispute concerns how this mechanism interacts with telecommunications licensing regulations.

So here is a question worth answering.

Which produces the greater measurable public benefit?

A prescribed ownership structure?

Or a legally enforceable programme providing educational infrastructure to thousands of underserved schools?

Ownership can provide real empowerment when beneficiaries exercise genuine control and receive economic value.

Infrastructure investment can provide real empowerment when it reaches communities and delivers sustained benefits.

Neither should be accepted merely because somebody attaches the word "transformation" to it.

Both should be judged by results.

And now consider another interesting feature of South African law.

In June 2008, the Pretoria High Court ruled that qualifying Chinese South Africans fell within the statutory definition of black people for BEE and employment-equity purposes.

That was a legal ruling concerning eligibility under South Africa's race-based empowerment framework.

It did not extend automatic BEE status to Chinese foreign nationals or corporations.

But consider the commercial possibilities.

A qualifying Chinese South African owns a local BEE-compliant company.

The company imports equipment from relatives or commercial partners in China.

The South African business may benefit from its empowerment status while its overseas suppliers receive revenue from the relationship.

That can be a perfectly lawful international family business.

Now introduce South Africa's well-documented history of BEE fronting, politically connected intermediaries and corruption in public procurement.

Suddenly, the questions become rather more interesting.

Who actually owns the local company?

Who finances it?

Who exercises voting control?

Who supplies its products?

Who receives the profits?

Are related-party prices commercially reasonable?

And are politically connected individuals influencing the award of government contracts?

The B-BBEE Commission has investigated invalid ownership claims involving Chinese foreign nationals.

The State Capture Commission documented how political connections, intermediary companies and public procurement were exploited for private enrichment.

Neither finding proves wrongdoing by a particular Chinese-linked company.

But they demonstrate why an empowerment certificate should never substitute for examining beneficial ownership and the flow of money.

A genuine local shareholder is one thing.

A figurehead concealing foreign control is another.

A competitive supplier is one thing.

An intermediary receiving an undisclosed commission for political access is another.

And a legitimately awarded government contract is not the same as a contract steered towards politically connected interests.

Follow the shareholders.

Follow the procurement decisions.

Follow the related-party transactions.

Follow the money.

Because a Trojan horse does not always need soldiers hidden inside.

Sometimes the company is already registered locally, the merchandise arrives from overseas, the intermediary knows the right people, and the state pays the invoice.

That possibility is worth investigating, whether the companies involved are Chinese, American, European or South African.

The Other Billionaire's Network

George Soros began philanthropic activities in South Africa in 1979.

The Open Society Foundation for South Africa was established in 1993, just before the country's political transition.

Over subsequent decades, Open Society supported initiatives involving journalism, education, research, legal advocacy, public health and civil society.

Its own publications document substantial funding of South African institutions.

Some of that work produced important results, including support for investigative journalism and public accountability.

That should not be ignored.

But neither should the influence associated with sustained institutional funding.

A donor need not dictate an editor's headline to influence which journalism projects can be financed.

A donor need not instruct a lecturer on what to teach to influence which research programmes receive support.

And a donor need not control an organisation to help determine whether that organisation possesses the resources to expand.

Funding matters.

Institutional networks matter.

Long-term relationships matter.

That is precisely why political and philanthropic organisations invest in them.

There is no verified evidence that Open Society directly controls South Africa's media, universities or governing party.

But there is documented evidence of extensive involvement in civil society, research, advocacy and media development.

Why shouldn't those financial relationships receive scrutiny when the subject is foreign influence?

And Open Society's international record is not limited to South Africa.

In Serbia, Soros-funded organisations supported independent media and civil-society activity before the removal of Slobodan Milošević in 2000.

In Georgia, Open Society-supported institutions were part of the civic environment surrounding the Rose Revolution in 2003.

In Ukraine, the International Renaissance Foundation began operating in 1990, before the country's independence from the Soviet Union.

Its activities subsequently included legal reform, investigative journalism, anti-corruption work and civil-society support.

Some of these organisations were active during major political upheavals.

The evidence does not establish that Soros personally engineered those revolutions. Domestic grievances, political decisions and local movements were fundamental.

But the foreign financing is documented.

It is part of the history.

And that makes Eaton's selective concern about billionaire influence worth questioning.

One wealthy man proposes to finance satellite infrastructure.

Another has spent decades financing civic institutions that participate in public debate and political reform.

These are different forms of influence.

Both deserve transparency.

Yet somehow only one becomes a Trojan horse.

Perhaps it is easier to warn people about a satellite dish than to explain the influence of grants, advocacy networks and institutional funding.

The dish is visible.

The grant agreements require reading.

The ANC Signs. South Africans Pay.

Foreign influence also arrives through something considerably less entertaining than a wooden horse.

Loan agreements.

In July 2026, South Africa signed a US$1.5 billion development-policy loan with the World Bank.

It was the fourth such loan since 2022.

The financing supports agreed reforms involving electricity, freight transport, water and sanitation.

Development-policy lending is linked to negotiated policy and institutional actions.

These conditions are documented.

There is no need to imagine secret instructions being delivered to Pretoria.

The government negotiates the agreement.

The lender evaluates the agreed reforms.

The funds are disbursed according to the applicable arrangements.

And the repayment obligations remain with South Africa.

Some reforms may be necessary. Some may deliver substantial benefits.

But the arrangement illustrates the influence international financing institutions can exercise over national policy.

South Africa voluntarily enters these agreements, meaning responsibility remains with its elected government.

That is the crucial point.

The ANC-led administration makes the commitments.

International lenders monitor agreed measures.

National Treasury services the debt.

And South Africans bear the financial consequences.

When the repayment obligations come due, the invoice does not arrive at Luthuli House.

It arrives in the national accounts.

The G20 itself is not a lender, but major G20 economies influence the international financial system through which institutions such as the World Bank operate.

The important distinction is between international financial influence and direct political control.

One does not automatically prove the other.

But financing arrangements still deserve scrutiny.

If the government must borrow to address failures in electricity, freight, water and other services, citizens are entitled to know what commitments have been made and what results those commitments produce.

The ANC signs.

South Africans live with the consequences.

And when implementation fails, accountability cannot simply be transferred to the lender.

Yet we are asked to believe that a proposed satellite connection to a rural school is the foreign relationship most urgently deserving suspicion.

It is certainly the one providing Eaton with the most material for jokes.

Ukraine: The Part That Deserves Serious Scrutiny

Eaton's strongest criticism involves the restrictions affecting Ukrainian military use of SpaceX satellite services.

And unlike some of the column's more theatrical arguments, this is serious.

October 2026 reporting indicates that restrictions disrupted Ukrainian military and drone operations.

The Wall Street Journal and CBS News reported that Musk was involved in ordering the restrictions. Reuters also reported accounts attributing responsibility to the White House, illustrating unresolved questions about the decision-making process.

Ukrainian officials have said the disruption created operational risks.

These developments deserve investigation and accountability.

Critical military systems should not depend on arrangements whose operational limitations can be changed without adequate protection for those relying on them.

But Eaton moves from allegations and reporting about a particular operational decision to sweeping conclusions about Musk's motives and moral character.

That leap needs more evidence.

Starlink also supplied important communications capabilities to Ukraine after Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022.

That history should not be erased.

Nor should the more recent controversy be dismissed.

Both matter.

And there is a further distinction.

Military satellite communications used during an active war are not identical to a regulated commercial broadband service for South African schools.

The operational requirements, contractual relationships and risks differ.

South Africa should insist on protections against arbitrary interruption.

It should demand security standards, enforceable commitments and competitive alternatives.

Those safeguards should apply to Starlink.

They should apply to Chinese satellite operators.

And they should apply to every other provider seeking access to South Africa's communications market.

That is a consistent principle.

A columnist's personal opinion of Musk is not a substitute for one.

The Bozell and Trump Distractions

Eaton also turns his attention to American ambassador Brent Bozell.

His hairstyle.

His conservative politics.

His past activism.

And, inevitably, Donald Trump.

It makes for lively copy.

But the central regulatory question remains unchanged.

Should qualifying foreign satellite operators be permitted to use equity-equivalent investment mechanisms under a clearly defined licensing framework?

That question does not disappear because somebody dislikes the American ambassador.

Neither does it disappear because the ambassador misstated the number of schools proposed for connectivity.

Bozell said 500.

Starlink's published proposal says 5,000.

Correct the ambassador.

Then examine the proposal.

That would be the common-sense response.

Instead, we are invited into a sprawling debate about American politics.

Eaton also mocks claims concerning Afrikaner refugees by citing an alleged application percentage.

But without an established denominator, a confirmed number of eligible applicants and reliable application data, such a percentage cannot carry the rhetorical weight he places on it.

More importantly, refugee claims are not decided by the popularity of the applicants or the commentator's political preferences.

They require individual assessments under the applicable legal standards.

And whatever one's position on American immigration policy, none of this tells a rural South African school whether satellite connectivity is technically reliable, legally permissible or financially worthwhile.

For all the detours through Washington, the South African school remains precisely where it was.

Still waiting for the discussion to return to internet access.

The Real Trojan Horse Test

Eaton's metaphor works because the Trojan horse represents a gift concealing a danger.

That is a legitimate concept.

Governments should scrutinise foreign corporations providing critical infrastructure.

They should examine ownership, service continuity, cybersecurity, contractual authority and national security.

But the same commitment to transparency must extend beyond the companies a columnist happens to dislike.

South Africa already has foreign-funded advocacy organisations.

International technology partnerships.

Development financing agreements.

And public procurement systems exposed to political interference and corruption.

These are different arrangements with different risks.

They should not be bundled together as evidence of a single secret conspiracy.

But they all raise questions about influence and accountability.

Who supplies the money?

What conditions are attached?

Who controls the assets?

Who shapes the decisions?

Who receives the benefits?

And who pays when the arrangement fails?

Those are the questions worth asking.

Not whether Elon Musk is sufficiently charming to deserve permission to operate a satellite network.

And certainly not whether a privileged commentator finds the billionaire politically fashionable.

After more than thirty years of ANC-led government, South Africans are entitled to expect functioning public institutions.

Reliable electricity.

Working railways.

Safe communities.

Clean water.

Employment opportunities.

And schools equipped for the modern world.

Those are not radical demands.

They are basic expectations.

Yet too often the public is offered ideological arguments in place of measurable results.

A familiar pattern emerges.

The institutions fail.

Politicians explain.

Commentators defend or attack according to political loyalty.

And ordinary people are expected to accept another lecture about what is supposedly good for them.

The same ordinary people who have no meaningful influence over the discussion.

The same people whose schools may lack connectivity.

The same people for whom another year of failed delivery is not an intellectual debate but everyday life.

So investigate Musk.

Investigate Chinese satellite operators.

Examine Huawei's government partnerships.

Examine Open Society's funding.

Read the World Bank loan agreements.

Scrutinise BEE ownership and state procurement.

And apply the same standards to everybody.

As for Eaton's Trojan horse?

Foreign influence did not begin when Starlink approached South Africa.

It has operated openly for decades through foundations, financing arrangements, corporate partnerships and public institutions.

The grants have records.

The contracts have signatures.

The loans have repayment schedules.

The money has a trail.

Perhaps the next Trojan horse should arrive carrying a more reassuring sign:

"DEFINITELY DOES NOT CONTAIN FOREIGN INFLUENCE."

Preferably accompanied by an approved grant, an academic endorsement and a conference on protecting democracy.

Then nobody will think to look inside.

Meanwhile, somewhere in rural South Africa, children are still waiting for reliable internet.

But at least the people who already have it have explained why they should be suspicious of the man offering it.

How reassuring.

Perhaps the greatest irony is that Eaton's argument is supposedly about protecting ordinary South Africans.

Yet the ordinary South Africans who might benefit from the service barely feature in the discussion.

The privileged have the connectivity.

The privileged have the platforms.

And the privileged apparently know what everyone else should want.

Common sense, Mr Eaton, might begin by asking the people without internet what they actually need.

Rather than deciding for them through your ideological spectacles.

Because the issue was never simply whether to let a wooden horse through the gates.

It was whether South Africa would finally apply the same scrutiny to the power already inside.
