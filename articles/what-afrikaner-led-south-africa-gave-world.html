What Afrikaner-Led South Africa Gave the World

1948–1994 | The inventions, engineering and ingenuity that travelled far beyond our borders.

In 2015, American engineers repaired Cleveland Harbour's breakwater using 16,813 enormous concrete structures, each weighing approximately 6.5 tonnes. They were dolosse, the interlocking concrete units developed in East London, South Africa, in 1963 by engineer Eric Mowbray Merrifield and his team. Their distinctive shape helps dissipate wave energy while allowing water to pass through. They became an influential tool in coastal engineering around the world.

Engineering that travelled

Dolosse are one example of South African ingenuity that moved well beyond the country's borders. The period from 1948 to 1994 saw important advances in engineering, mining, energy, medicine and industrial design. Some were developed under Afrikaner-led governments; many involved English-speaking South Africans, other communities, immigrants and international collaborators. The national achievement is real, and contributions should be attributed accurately.

Defence engineering and mine safety

The Milkor MGL, a six-shot 40 mm multiple grenade launcher developed in South Africa, became one of the country's best-known defence exports. South African engineers also developed the Husky vehicle for detecting and surviving landmine blasts. Its specialised mine-detection role made it useful in humanitarian demining and military route clearance.

Medical innovation

The first human heart transplant, performed by Christiaan Barnard's team at Groote Schuur Hospital on 3 December 1967, remains a defining moment in modern medicine. It was a team achievement involving surgeons, nurses, anaesthetists, technicians and the wider hospital staff.

Surgeon Marius Barnard helped develop the concept of critical illness insurance after recognising that surviving a major illness could leave patients facing serious financial hardship. The product category spread internationally and became a significant part of the insurance industry.

Materials, machines and everyday inventions

Pratley Putty, developed by George Pratley, was used in the electrical assembly of NASA's Ranger 9 spacecraft. Ranger 9 was an uncrewed lunar probe that impacted the Moon in 1965. The Kreepy Krauly automatic pool cleaner, developed in South Africa by Ferdinand Chauvier, became a globally recognised household product.

Industrial science and energy

BIOX, a bio-oxidation process developed through South African research, helped make certain refractory gold ores more amenable to conventional recovery and was adopted internationally. The CSIR's Heavy Vehicle Simulator helped engineers test road pavements under accelerated loading. The Tellurometer, developed by Dr Trevor Wadley and colleagues, transformed long-distance electronic distance measurement and influenced modern surveying practice.

Sasol's coal-to-liquids technology represented a major industrial engineering undertaking, converting coal into liquid fuels and associated chemical products. Its history is tied to the strategic circumstances of the period, but the technical expertise and industrial systems developed in South Africa had a lasting legacy.

A record worth remembering accurately

These achievements should not be reduced to a claim that one community alone invented everything. South African innovation was produced by individuals, research institutions, companies, universities and teams whose members came from different backgrounds. The role of Afrikaner-led governments in building or supporting parts of the country's industrial and scientific capacity is an important part of that story, but attribution matters.

The point is not to claim ownership where the record is shared. It is to ensure that the record is not forgotten. Dolosse still protect coastlines. South African medical history still features in textbooks. Engineering systems developed here continue to influence industries abroad. That is a legacy worth knowing, documenting and passing on.
