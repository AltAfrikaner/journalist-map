The Church That Isn't a Church

Why this programme threatens the people fighting it

But the Afrikaner story was never really about Afrikaners alone. Afrikaners are a small community, roughly two million people, who happened to represent everything the progressive institutional world needed a villain for. They are Christian. They are Western. They built a functioning state, an independent judiciary, a nuclear programme, a first-world economy on the tip of Africa. They defended national sovereignty against a Soviet-backed insurgency. In other words, they represented the traditional West in miniature, on a continent where that representation could be isolated, targeted, and dismantled without the rest of the Western world noticing, or caring, until it was too late.

That is why the Afrikaner case matters far beyond South Africa. Traditional values become "far-right." National borders become "exclusion." Christianity becomes "Christian nationalism." Meritocracy becomes "systemic privilege." The vocabulary changes depending on the country, but the method is identical: take a group that built something, redefine its history as oppression, and then use that redefinition to dismantle everything the group represents. Afrikaners were the test case. The template worked. It is now being applied everywhere.

The US refugee programme does something simple that threatens all of it. It treats Afrikaners as people who might be victims of persecution. If that idea gains international legitimacy, it does not merely reverse one classification. And it asks the one question that the institutions documented on this page have spent years and hundreds of millions of dollars making sure nobody asks: what if the people you were told were the villains are actually in danger?

That is the context for everything that follows. The organisations opposing this programme are not simply disagreeing with an immigration policy. When white South African families apply for refugee status in the United States, their applications are processed by an organisation called Church World Service, or CWS. Most people hear the word "church" and picture a religious charity running on donations. CWS is nothing of the sort. The CWS homepage: "We have one goal: building a world where there is enough for all." For 80 years. With $520 million in federal grants since 2021.

CWS describes itself as a cooperative ministry of 37 Christian denominations. In practice, it operates as a quarter-billion-dollar-a-year government contractor. It holds a cooperative agreement with the US State Department's Bureau of Population, Refugees, and Migration (PRM). Through this contract, it runs the Resettlement Support Center (RSC) for Africa, headquartered in Nairobi, Kenya, with a sub-office in Pretoria, South Africa. The RSC is the processing centre that prepares refugee case files, arranges medical examinations, schedules interviews, and books travel for every sub-Saharan African refugee heading to the United States. There is no other organisation doing this work. CWS is the only option. It has held this position continuously since 1989.

The conflict of interest

The core problem

Picture an Afrikaner family. They have been approved as refugees. They sold their house, quit their jobs, pulled their children from school. Their case is being processed by Church World Service. Now picture the same organisation in a federal courtroom, arguing that this family's entire programme is illegal, discriminatory, and rooted in white supremacy. That is not a hypothetical. It is documented reality.

On 7 February 2025, President Trump signed Executive Order 14204 creating a refugee programme for white South Africans facing race-based violence and discrimination. The programme is commonly called the "Afrikaner refugee programme" although it covers white South Africans more broadly. Three days later, CWS became a named plaintiff in a federal lawsuit called Pacito v. Trump, filed in the Western District of Washington (Seattle), challenging the legality of the refugee suspension and later the Afrikaner preference specifically. On 7 March, the State Department told CWS to resume processing South African cases. CWS agreed the same day.

CEO Rick Santos called the programme a "moral failure" and said it was "steeped in an ideology of white supremacy." CWS's official Racial Justice Platform declares that "white supremacy and misogyny continue to target and destroy the lives and communities of BIPOC." The organisation's own doctrine treats white claims of persecution as extensions of the supremacy CWS exists to dismantle.

On 7 April 2026, CWS's lawyers filed an amended complaint specifically targeting the Afrikaner preference. The organisation processing every Afrikaner case was simultaneously litigating to make those cases illegal.

Families stranded

The human cost

The programme created under Executive Order 14204 is commonly described as the "Afrikaner refugee programme," but its scope extends to white South Africans more broadly. Approved families, Afrikaans and English-speaking, reported the same pattern: tickets issued, belongings sold, jobs resigned, children pulled from school. Then last-minute cancellations with no clear timeline. Medical clearances expired while families waited. Security checks lapsed. No explanation was provided. Families who had given up everything were left with nothing.

Why CWS cannot simply be shut down

CWS (Church World Service) is not a government agency like USAID (United States Agency for International Development). It is a private nonprofit. The government cannot disband it. What the government can do is terminate the cooperative agreement and award it to someone else, or bring the function in-house. But CWS has held the RSC (Resettlement Support Center) Africa contract since 1989, building all the infrastructure: the Nairobi headquarters, the staff, the processing systems, the medical examination networks. No other organisation has this capability for 49 African countries. Terminating CWS tomorrow would stop refugee processing entirely.

The Trump administration appears to recognise this. It designated a separate organisation called Amerikaners as a State Department referral partner, handling identification and initial screening. CWS still controls the back end: documentation, medicals, and travel. The administration may be building a parallel capability while keeping CWS operational and observable. A functioning machine is easier to map than a dismantled one.

The Anglican chain

Why this matters

To understand this section, you need to know one thing about South African church history. The Afrikaner community is historically Dutch Reformed. They were never part of the Anglican communion. The Anglican Church in South Africa served the English-speaking and Black communities, and its leaders were central to the movement that fought against apartheid and the Afrikaner political establishment. That fight is history. But the institutions that waged it did not dissolve when apartheid ended. They survived. The personnel connections survived. The commitments survived. And that institutional network now extends from the Archbishop of Cape Town, through the American Episcopal Church (which is the US branch of the Anglican communion), into Church World Service. The family in the RSC Africa processing queue is being handled by the institutional descendants of the movement that opposed their community for eighty years.

In 1943, Anglican priest Trevor Huddleston arrived at Christ the King in Sophiatown. He befriended Mandela and Oliver Tambo. Tambo nearly became an Anglican priest before politics consumed him. He led the ANC in exile for thirty years.

In 1959, Huddleston co-founded the Anti-Apartheid Movement in London alongside the Communist Party of Great Britain. Anglican clergy provided moral authority. The Communist Party provided operational infrastructure.

Decades later, Thabo Makgoba served at the same Sophiatown parish. He became Archbishop of Cape Town in 2007, succeeding Njongonkulu Ndungane (a Robben Island prisoner), who had succeeded Desmond Tutu.

From institutional alignment to operational impact

Everything up to this point has been about who sits on which board, who funds whom, who shares which ideology. This section is different. This is about a decision that directly hurt Afrikaner families. CWS had 100 trained South African staff who knew the context, spoke the languages, and had valid work authorisation. It fired them. When the Afrikaner programme created demand, CWS chose not to rehire any of them. Instead it flew in Kenyans on the wrong visas, creating a vulnerability that the South African government exploited to shut down processing. Whether this was incompetence or design, the result was the same: families stranded.

In June 2024, CWS closed its Pretoria office and retrenched approximately 100 South African staff. Seven months later, Trump's EO created massive processing demand. CWS did not rehire locally. It applied for volunteer visas to bring 30 Kenyans from Nairobi. Volunteer visas require unpaid work. CWS was advertising salaried positions. Home Affairs denied the visas. CWS applied for work permits. Denied again.

Despite two formal refusals, the Kenyans entered on tourist visas and set up in a rented guest house in Pretoria. On 16 December 2025, authorities raided the centre. Seven arrested, deported, banned for five years. Processing disrupted.

The media framed this as South Africa attacking a US operation. Nobody asked: why did CWS deploy visa-vulnerable foreign workers into a programme the host government was fighting, instead of rehiring the 100 trained South Africans it had fired six months earlier?

IRAP has been spending down that war chest ever since, running annual deficits while ramping up litigation and advocacy. The Ford Foundation separately provided IRAP with a $100,000 grant in October 2025, active during the period when the amended Pacito complaint targeting Afrikaners was being prepared.

On 7 March 2025, the State Department directed CWS to resume RSC Africa operations for the Afrikaner programme. CWS concurred the same day. The organisation agreed to run the programme it was simultaneously suing to end. It needed the contract to survive financially. Walking away would have meant losing its last remaining federal cooperative agreement.

The funding pipeline through South Africa

Two channels from one government

This is where the money trail gets unexpected. The US State Department funds CWS directly through one bureau (PRM, the Bureau of Population, Refugees, and Migration) to process refugees. But a different bureau in the same State Department (DRL, the Bureau of Democracy, Human Rights, and Labor) also funded a separate South African organisation called The Other Foundation, through a programme called the Global Equality Fund (GEF). The Other Foundation then made grants to CWS in South Africa for church and refugee work. Two separate channels from the same government department converge on the same organisation. The Other Foundation also received money from the Open Society Foundations (George Soros), the Ford Foundation, and other progressive philanthropies. It partnered with Facebook's Africa office and placed a journalist inside the Mail and Guardian newsroom in Johannesburg.

Six strands, one machine

The full picture

This investigation traced six institutional strands. Each has its own history, personnel, and funding. Individually, any strand could be dismissed as coincidence. Together, they form a single ecosystem where every node that touches an Afrikaner refugee family is connected to every other node. The machine was not built to stop Afrikaner refugees. It was built across three US presidencies for other purposes and activated when Trump's Executive Order created the trigger.

Anglican

Huddleston founded the AAM (Anti-Apartheid Movement). Tambo was Anglican. Makgoba consulted Rowe. The Episcopal Church is a CWS Covenant Member. The WCC (World Council of Churches) funded liberation movements. CWS's parent body is the NCC (National Council of Churches).

Communist

The CPGB (Communist Party of Great Britain) co-built the AAM with Huddleston. The NCC absorbed its predecessor, the Federal Council of Churches, which had roots in the Fabian-influenced Christian Socialist movement. Kasrils (SACP Central Committee) sits on the Coalition for Good council alongside WCC-connected figures.

Intelligence / DIRCO

Kasrils headed ANC military intelligence and served as Minister of Intelligence Services. Pandor oversaw DIRCO (Department of International Relations and Cooperation). Dangor, her former special advisor, became DIRCO Director-General and facilitated the Palestinian admission and the diplomatic opposition to the Afrikaner programme.

ANC government

CWS funded the SACC (South African Council of Churches, $280K in 1986). CWS's archive contains a dedicated ANC file from 1986-1988. Pandor, Chikane, and Makgoba all hold ANC-aligned institutional positions. Rasool's expulsion activated the Makgoba ecclesiastical channel.

US Democrats

IRAP's board included Power, Finer, and McDonough (all entered the Biden government). Perkins Coie has served as the Democratic Party's principal election law firm. Brigety was an Obama/Biden appointee throughout his career in government and diplomacy.

What the investigation proved

The evidence standard

Every claim on this page is sourced from audited financial statements, IRS 990 filings, company records, UK Companies House filings, USAspending.gov, court documents, official press releases, and published reporting. Where evidence is absent, that gap is named. No connection is asserted without a documentary trail.

Every entity that touches the Afrikaner refugee pipeline shares personnel, funding sources, institutional memory, and stated commitments with the network that opposed the Afrikaner community for eighty years.

Every family passing through RSC Africa encounters an organisation whose leadership has publicly described their programme as a moral failure, whose board includes the former federal official who supervised its contract, whose legal counsel is governed by Ford Foundation and Open Society Foundations personnel, and whose official Racial Justice Platform frames the conceptual category into which Afrikaners fall in terms that preclude sympathy.

The reader can assess what this means.

The wider funding architecture

The Ford Foundation and Open Society Foundations jointly created a $25 million fund specifically for South African civil society groups advancing constitutionalism. Ford's president described sixty years of supporting South African civil society "in their quest to make the promise of democracy a reality." This is the same constitutional framework that the ANC government uses to reject the premise that Afrikaners face persecution: affirmative action is not discrimination, it is constitutional correction of historical injustice.

Separately, CWS received approximately $20 million from FEMA (Federal Emergency Management Agency) for "countering violent extremism" programmes. CWS's own Racial Justice Platform frames "white supremacy" as the defining threat it exists to combat. Whether the conceptual frameworks from CWS's counter-extremism work have influenced how the organisation views the Afrikaner programme is an open question.
