The FW de Klerk Foundation: From Watchdog to Chorus Line

For 24 years, the Foundation named after South Africa's last apartheid-era president held the ANC government accountable for eroding the Constitution. Then, in January 2024, a new leader was appointed. Within eighteen months, the Foundation was calling Afrikaner persecution claims "lies" and telling America's ambassador to stop asking questions.

Alt Afrikaner · May 2026

The Foundation in Brief

The FW de Klerk Foundation was established in 1999 by former President FW de Klerk (Nobel Peace Laureate, died November 2021) and his chief of staff Dave Steward (former Director-General of the Office of the President) to defend the South African Constitution and monitor whether the settlement they negotiated was being honoured.

In January 2024, Christo van der Rheede was appointed Executive Director, replacing the retiring Steward. What followed was a measurable shift in the Foundation's mission, leadership, public positioning, and institutional alliances.

Part One

What the Foundation Was

Before we can understand what changed, you need to know what existed. The FW de Klerk Foundation was not a ceremonial legacy project. It was the sharpest institutional critic of the ANC government operating from within the constitutional framework.

Dave Steward was not a figurehead attached to a famous man's name. He was the Director-General of the Office of the President during the constitutional negotiations of the early 1990s, the man who sat across the table from the ANC's team and understood, with the clarity that only proximity provides, what the ANC intended to do with the state once it held power.

When Steward and De Klerk established the Foundation in 1999, they gave it a single analytical mission: monitor whether the constitutional settlement they had negotiated was being honoured by the government that inherited it. Steward's conclusion, developed over twenty-four years of forensic analysis, was that it was not. He documented how the ANC's guiding ideology, the National Democratic Revolution (NDR), treated the Constitution not as a binding agreement but as a tactical advance to be superseded by revolutionary transformation.

The Foundation's output under Steward was dense and uncomfortable. It traced how ANC policy documents translated into legislation: the Expropriation Bill (allowing land seizure without compensation), the Employment Equity Act (imposing racial quotas on businesses), the National Health Insurance Bill (restructuring healthcare along ideological lines). It produced the annual Human Rights Report Card, grading the government's constitutional performance. It engaged President Thabo Mbeki directly in three rounds of formal dialogue between 2002 and 2005.

In his final years at the Foundation, Steward reviewed Anthea Jeffery's Countdown to Socialism and used it to dismantle President Ramaphosa's "radical second phase" of the NDR with forensic precision, quoting ANC Strategy and Tactics documents and SACP General Secretary Jeremy Cronin's own critiques. This was the Foundation's signature: using the ANC's own words against its own programme.

That entire body of work has now been eliminated from the Foundation's analytical production. Not refuted. Not debated. Eliminated.

Part Two

The Crisis That Hollowed Out the Leadership

In February 2020, a single media statement triggered a chain of resignations that removed every senior leader from the Foundation. By the time the new director was appointed four years later, the institutional memory was gone.

On Valentine's Day 2020, the Foundation described the United Nations' classification of apartheid as a crime against humanity as "Soviet agitprop." The statement was historically grounded: the designation originated from a 1966 UN General Assembly resolution and a 1973 Convention, both driven by the Soviet Union. It was also politically catastrophic. Within days, the Foundation withdrew and apologised under enormous public pressure.

The aftermath was an institutional haemorrhage. CEO Dayne Morkel resigned in March 2020 and moved to a dormant US entity, the Foundation for Peace in Divided Societies (a 501(c)(3) shell in Richmond, Virginia, that still lists the deceased De Klerk as chairman and reports annual receipts below $50,000). Board chairman Theuns Eloff resigned in April 2020. Steward returned as acting Executive Director in January 2022, but announced his retirement in December 2023.

The Foundation that the new director inherited in January 2024 had been emptied of everyone who understood its original mission. The constitutional-defence DNA, the NDR expertise, the negotiating-era institutional memory: all had departed with Steward. What remained was the name, the constitutional programme staff, and a German partnership.

Part Three

The Man They Chose

Christo van der Rheede was appointed Executive Director in January 2024. His background is fundamentally different from any previous Foundation leader. Understanding his career path explains why the Foundation changed direction.

Van der Rheede is a Coloured South African from the Cape Flats, educated at the University of the Western Cape (UWC) and Stellenbosch University. He began his career as a music teacher at a Cape Flats primary school. He holds Royal School of Music diplomas in Advanced Piano and Singing. Nothing in his background connects to constitutional law, NDR analysis, or the negotiating history that defined the Foundation's mission.

What his background does connect to is a specific career pattern. Every executive position Van der Rheede held before the Foundation followed the same logic: a historically white Afrikaner institution, facing political pressure to demonstrate racial transformation, appointed a Coloured leader to de-racialise its public image while maintaining institutional survival.

2005 – 2011
CEO, Small Business Association
A trust sponsored by Media24 (Naspers), the former National Party press conglomerate. Naspers gave Van der Rheede his first executive credential.
2012 – 2015
CEO, Afrikaanse Handelsinstituut (AHI)
The Afrikaner business chamber, founded by the Broederbond in 1942. SA History Online recorded his appointment as "another coloured man" heading the organisation. At the AHI, he told Parliament that "the government's fixation with ideology is steering the country to disaster."
2015 – 2023
Deputy CEO then CEO, AgriSA
The commercial farmers' union. He positioned farm attack statistics to show declining trends, aligning with the ANC government's preferred narrative. His credential as "former CEO of South Africa's biggest agricultural organisation" became the weapon he later deployed at the Foundation.
January 2024 – Present
Executive Director, FW de Klerk Foundation
The same pattern, applied to the highest-stakes institution: de-racialise the Foundation, align with the dominant consensus, avoid positions that invite political attack.

The paradox is revealing. At the AHI in 2015, Van der Rheede attacked the ANC's ideology and threatened to cut its funding. At the Foundation in 2025, he told eNCA that claims of Afrikaner persecution were "all lies" and called Afrikaner advocacy groups a "right-wing fringe." The same man delivered opposite messages because different institutions required different positioning for survival.

"Us representing the Afrikaans community, we are very adamant that there is no such thing happening. These are all lies."

Christo van der Rheede, eNCA interview, November 2025

Part Five

Five Networks Control the Foundation

The Foundation is not controlled by any single actor. It is controlled by five overlapping networks that all require the same outcome: the presentation of South Africa's democratic transition as a success. None of these networks needs to coordinate. They all point in the same direction because they all depend on the same narrative.

Network 1: The Pipeline

UWC-Cape Flats Career Escalator

The University of the Western Cape, under Rector Jakes Gerwel, trained a generation of Coloured professionals for post-apartheid institutional roles. Franklin Sonn (UWC alumnus, ANC ambassador) was the prototype. Van der Rheede (UWC alumnus, Foundation ED) is the second generation. The Foundation's 2025 Goodwill Award went to Sonn: it honoured its own DNA transplant.

Network 2: The Credential

Naspers/Media24 Funding Chain

Van der Rheede's first CEO role was at a Media24 (Naspers) trust. Naspers, the old National Party press, repositioned after 1994 and now funds transformation within historically Afrikaner institutions. Each subsequent appointment (AHI, AgriSA, Foundation) used the credential from the previous one.

Network 3: The Money

KAS/German Government Framework

Christina Teichmann spent 20+ years at KAS. KAS follows BMZ guidelines. Germany committed €276 million to SA for 2024-2026. KAS co-hosts every Foundation conference. The BMZ describes South Africa as "a stable democracy." The Foundation validates that claim. If the model is shown to have failed, Germany's 30-year investment collapses.

Network 4: The Lock

NFDI Foundation Alliance

The Foundation sits alongside the Mbeki, Tutu, Luthuli, Tambo, Biko, Sobukwe, and Gerwel foundations in the National Foundations Dialogue Initiative. Any analysis that challenges the ANC's narrative on minority safety would rupture these relationships. The NFDI is the consensus lock that prevents the Foundation from returning to its original mission.

Network 5: The Shield

Bakala/Czech Liberal Philanthropy

Michaela Bakala connects the Foundation to the Aspen Institute and Václav Havel Library. The Bakala family's money built the Foundation's physical centre. Their worldview treats populist-national movements (including Trump) as threats to institutional stability, matching the Foundation's anti-Trump positioning precisely.

Part Six

Why Germany Needs This to Work

The Foundation's capture is not a domestic South African story alone. It serves an international function. Germany has invested billions of euros over thirty years in the narrative that South Africa's democratic transition succeeded. The Foundation, carrying a Nobel laureate's name, validates that narrative at a moment when it is under unprecedented pressure.

Germany is the world's second-largest bilateral development donor. In November 2024, Berlin committed €276.5 million in new funding to South Africa. The BMZ, Germany's development ministry, describes South Africa as having "developed into a stable democracy" with "a very progressive constitution."

That is the official narrative justifying three decades of German investment. If the South African model is shown to have produced a state that discriminates against its minorities through 118 race-based laws, pursues demographic redistribution of property and employment, and presides over conditions where farming communities face persistent violent attack, then the entire Western framework for managing post-authoritarian multicultural transitions is discredited. Germany's own reunification process, its refugee integration programme, and its EU diversity leadership all draw on assumptions that South Africa's experience validated.

The FW de Klerk Foundation validates that narrative with unique credibility. When the Foundation named after the man who dismantled apartheid says the GNU is working, that farm attacks are not racial, that international concern is "alarmism," no other institution can match that authority. The ANC cannot say it credibly because it is the accused party. Only De Klerk's Foundation can.

That is why the institutional capture matters beyond South African borders. The Foundation is not merely serving the ANC government's domestic messaging. It is sustaining an international credibility function that justifies continued European investment in a model that may already have failed.

Verdict

The Foundation Still Defends the Constitution

The Foundation has not become incompetent or corrupt. Its constitutional legal staff still produce rigorous work on property rights and language rights. What has changed is narrower and more damaging than that.

The Foundation still defends the Constitution on property rights and language rights. It just no longer defends the people the Constitution was designed to protect.

Alt Afrikaner

The mechanism was not conspiracy. It was convergence. The 2020 crisis removed every leader who understood the original mission. The board's composition guaranteed a replacement who would prioritise institutional survival over analytical independence. The German partnership required validation of the "stable democracy" narrative. The NFDI alliance required consensus with ANC-adjacent foundations. The new director brought stakeholder-management instincts, not constitutional-warfare instincts.

Nobody needed to direct the process. The ANC's NDR did not need to plant an agent. It only needed to create conditions in which the Foundation's own survival instincts would produce an outcome indistinguishable from capture: an institution that now tells the world, with a Nobel laureate's authority, that the concerns of the people it was built to protect are "simply not true."

Dave Steward, the co-founder, continues to write from BizNews everything the Foundation was created to produce. His name remains on the Foundation's board as Chairperson Emeritus. His analysis and the Foundation's positioning now contradict each other on every politically significant question.

The Foundation carries the name. It no longer carries the mission.
