Before You Read The Rest

In plain language: what this page shows, and why it matters

1. What USAID was, in one paragraph. The United States Agency for International Development (USAID) was, until mid-2025, the American government's foreign-aid agency. It administered tens of billions of dollars annually across foreign assistance, including substantial funding for non-governmental organisations (NGOs), civil society, independent media, governance and elections work, humanitarian programmes and institutional capacity, in more than one hundred countries. Its funding decisions, its grantees and its budget were publicly filed, subject to Congressional oversight, and available to any citizen through a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. USAID had many defenders and many critics, but everyone agreed on one thing: whatever it did, it did in public, under law.

2. What replaced it, and where the Omidyar network fits. The Trump administration dismantled USAID and transferred its remaining foreign-assistance functions into the State Department beginning 1 July 2025. According to the USAID Office of Inspector General, more than 1,000 awards representing approximately USD 45 billion in development and humanitarian commitments were transferred. But the causes USAID had been funding, the local newsrooms, the fact-checkers, the refugee-legal organisations, did not stop working. Many of these organisations continue to receive substantial private philanthropic support, including from the Omidyar network and its peers (Ford Foundation, Open Society Foundations, MacArthur, Hewlett, Packard and others). Omidyar is one of the most extensively documented private networks operating across these fields. On this page it is discussed as a "private parallel" to the USAID model because, as Section 03 documents, USAID and private foundations (Omidyar entities among them) have co-financed or participated in the same governance infrastructure in at least three documented cases.

3. What the Omidyar network funds. Democracy Fund (their United States-facing foundation) has funded refugee-legal organisations including Church World Service (CWS) and the International Refugee Assistance Project (IRAP), which are the plaintiff and co-counsel in Pacito v. Trump, the case that in April 2026 was amended to challenge the Afrikaner-refugee preference specifically. Luminate (their global media-and-democracy arm) funds Africa Check, Media Monitoring Africa (MMA) and Code for Africa, whose assessments and reports have been incorporated into Meta's third-party fact-checking and moderation systems in Africa. MMA owns and operates Real411, the documented South African election-complaint platform that fed 289 complaints into the Electoral Commission of South Africa's Directorate for Electoral Offences during the 2024 elections. Humanity United (their peacebuilding foundation) has funded The Elders, the Desmond & Leah Tutu Legacy Foundation, and adjacent Anglican Communion institutions for two decades. The same worldwide Anglican Communion whose senior leaders publicly refused, in May 2025, to help resettle Afrikaner refugees in the United States. No causal link between the two has been established.

The Anglican Communion is the third-largest Christian body in the world, after the Roman Catholic Church and the Orthodox Church. Its American branch is the Episcopal Church. So "Episcopal" is simply the word Americans use for "Anglican." Archbishop Desmond Tutu was Anglican (Archbishop of Cape Town in the Anglican Church of Southern Africa). Archbishop Thabo Makgoba is Anglican (his current successor, installed as Archbishop of Cape Town in 2007). Presiding Bishop Sean Rowe is Anglican (head of the Episcopal Church, i.e. the American branch of the Anglican Communion). And Pierre Omidyar attended St Andrew's Episcopal School in Potomac, Maryland, an Anglican-tradition school formally governed within the Episcopal Diocese of Washington. Public records show the Omidyar Group has since donated approximately USD 625,000 back to that same school. Humanity United, the Omidyars' peacebuilding foundation, has been financially embedded in the Anglican-Tutu institutional world since 2007, including funding for the Tutu Legacy Foundation (documented in that foundation's 2022 annual report), Pam Omidyar personally financing a special issue of the Anglican Theological Review devoted to Tutu in 2022, and Humanity United being listed as a founding funder of Nelson Mandela's The Elders at their Johannesburg launch. 5. The Church World Service (CWS) connection, in plain English. Church World Service is a large American Christian refugee-resettlement charity. It is technically ecumenical (its member denominations include Methodists, Presbyterians, Lutherans, Reformed, and others), but the Episcopal Church (that is, the Anglican Church in the United States) is one of its most operationally significant Covenant Members, and Episcopal Migration Ministries (EMM) has a named representative on the CWS Faith Solidarity Team. Same city. Same one-month window. Two very different treatments of two very different refugee populations, delivered by an institutional ecosystem whose main foreign philanthropic funders (Open Society Foundations, Ford Foundation, and the Omidyar Group entities documented on this page) substantially overlap. The reader is left to draw their own conclusions.

02 · The Framework

The four channels of transnational institutional influence

To understand where the Omidyar architecture sits, and why it matters, it helps to first see the whole picture. There are four functional channels in the international governance ecosystem in 2026. This page calls them "channels" rather than "pillars" because they are not four coordinated components of one organisation. They are four independent but overlapping ways in which policy priorities, funding and personnel move across borders. Their downstream grantees, however, are frequently identical, and Section 03 documents three cases where the money literally converges on the same programme.

03 · Documented Intersections

12 · Editorial Standard

Honest limits: what is proven, what is not

What this investigation establishes

  • The corporate structure of the Omidyar Group and its ten constituent entities is public record, verified through the United States Internal Revenue Service Form 990 filings and the entities' own annual reports.
  • Specific dollar amounts named in this page, including Luminate to Africa Check (~8% of budget), Luminate to amaBhungane (USD 100,000+ plus rollover), Luminate to CODE-SA (USD 320,000), Omidyar Network Fund to World Economic Forum (USD 1.5M), Omidyar Network Fund to New Venture Fund (USD 800K), are sourced to grantee declarations, IRS filings, or the funders' own investment registers.
  • The corrected South African referral pipeline runs from a public complaint at Real411 (owned and operated by Media Monitoring Africa / Moxii Africa), to assessment by its Digital Complaints Committee (a body of legal, media and digital experts, without legally binding powers), to referral to the Electoral Commission's Directorate for Electoral Offences. This is documented in Real411's own procedural disclosures, the Electoral Commission's 2024 National and Provincial Elections Report, and the Partnership on Information Integrity in Elections (PIIE) memoranda. Africa Check operates in the adjacent information-integrity ecosystem but is not part of that specific serial referral process.
  • The dismantling of USAID in July 2025 and the subsequent operational continuity of its downstream grantee ecosystem is a matter of public record.

What this investigation does not claim

  • That any named individual has committed a criminal act.
  • That the Omidyar architecture specifically targeted the Afrikaner refugee question. The evidence supports a broader anti-Trump refugee-restructuring posture in which the Afrikaner pathway was one contested case among many. The record documents longstanding philanthropic relationships between Humanity United and parts of the Anglican and Tutu institutional ecosystem (The Elders, the Tutu Legacy Foundation, the Anglican Theological Review Tutu issue, Trinity Church Wall Street co-funding of the Tutu Legacy Foundation) and, separately, a documented Makgoba-Rowe consultation immediately preceding the Episcopal Church's May 2025 refusal to help resettle Afrikaner refugees. No causal bridge between those two facts has been established.
  • That any specific fact-check or platform removal has been improperly processed. The pipeline is described; individual outputs are not adjudicated here.
  • That funder-side influence over grantee editorial or litigation decisions has been directly proven. The infrastructure for influence is documented; the act of influence is not proven, and this distinction is preserved throughout.

Proportional scale, in context

  • The Omidyar architecture's roughly USD 100–150 million per year in grantmaking is small next to USAID's pre-2025 foreign-assistance budget of tens of billions of dollars annually. Its significance is not scale, but concentration: a small number of endowment-funded pooled vehicles align a small number of high-leverage NGOs on a small number of high-salience issues.
  • Nothing in this page should be read to suggest the philanthropic architecture is the only, or even the largest, influence on any given issue. The Ford Foundation, MacArthur Foundation, Open Society Foundations, and Rockefeller Philanthropy Advisors each match or exceed Omidyar in scale within specific programme areas.

Two open questions the record does not yet answer

  • Who chooses which complaints enter the Real411 intake queue on any given day, and by what criteria? The pipeline is public; the intake-gatekeeping is not.
  • What proportion of the 2026 Local Government Election disinformation-referral traffic will originate from partisan submissions rather than organic public reporting? The answer is measurable in principle, and not yet published.
